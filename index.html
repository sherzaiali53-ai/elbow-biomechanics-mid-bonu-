Effect of Object Distance from the Elbow on Estimated Biceps Force During Static Holding

Author

Shoxbonu Adilkhanova, Student ID: 260466

Objective

To develop a static-equilibrium model of the forearm and estimate how biceps force changes when an object is held at different distances from the elbow.

Background

The forearm (radius and ulna) rotates about the elbow joint. The biceps brachii attaches to the radius a few centimetres from the joint and pulls the forearm upward. A held object and the weight of the forearm both pull it downward. Because the biceps attaches so close to the pivot, it must produce a force much larger than the weight it holds.

This matters for understanding muscle and joint loading in lifting, carrying, rehabilitation and ergonomics. The model uses torque balance: when the arm is held still, the net torque about the elbow is zero.

Theoretical model

Forearm as a rigid lever. Biceps force F_b acts upward at distance a; forearm weight m·g acts at its centre of mass c; object weight M·g acts at distance d.

Assumptions

  • Forearm is a rigid lever held horizontal and motionless (static).
  • Elbow is a frictionless pivot.
  • Biceps force acts vertically at a fixed attachment distance.
  • Forearm and object weights act vertically downward.
  • Other muscles (co-contraction) are ignored.

Interpretation. For a fixed mass, F_b rises linearly with d: moving the object farther from the elbow increases the required biceps force in proportion to the object's moment arm. For a fixed distance, F_b rises linearly with M. Because a ≪ d, the biceps force is several times larger than the weight of the object (here, about 7 to 9 times for a 2 kg object at 0.30 m).

Evaluation

  • Method: hand calculations for several conditions, reproduced by the calculator above (spreadsheet or Python can be used to verify).
  • Sources of error: estimated segment mass, centre of mass and attachment distance vary between people; the biceps line of action is not exactly vertical.
  • Limitations: the model estimates one muscle force. It does not include co-contraction, changing muscle moment arms, or joint contact forces. Results are model estimates, not measured internal forces.
  • Future work: add elbow angle, compare with published EMG or musculoskeletal-model values.
